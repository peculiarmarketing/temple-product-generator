# Known Myths and Corrections

Claims that circulate widely as fact and must not ship. Several appear on government agency pages, university sites, and tour operator materials — repetition by a credible-looking source is not evidence.

When a corrected version is given, use the correction. When a claim is not listed here but has the same texture — a prophecy fulfilled by a building detail, an unsourced quotation, a too-perfect coincidence — treat it with the same suspicion and demand a primary citation.

## Kirtland Temple

**"The women of Kirtland donated their fine china to be crushed into the stucco."**
Unsourceable, and datable. The exterior stucco does contain crushed glass and ceramic, so the visible detail is real, but the fine-china version of the story does not appear in the historical record until 1940, over a century after the dedication. Contemporary accounts, including Artemus Millet's own reminiscence and his son's recollection, describe the material as "old glass and crockery," not china, and describe it being gathered rather than donated: children went through nearby towns and refuse heaps collecting broken dishes and glass to be ground into the mortar. Archaeological digs at the site have recovered period ceramic shards consistent with ordinary household refuse, not fine tableware.
*Correct version:* Master mason Artemus Millet mixed old glass and crockery into the exterior stucco to give the walls a shimmering finish. Children, including eight-year-old Elmeda Stringham Harmon, gathered the material. There is no record of anyone donating fine china for the purpose.

## Provo Utah Temple, 1972

**"The white base and gold spire were designed to symbolize the pillar of cloud by day and the pillar of fire by night that guided the Israelites in the Exodus."**
Retroactive folklore, not a design intent. Fred Baker, who worked closely with architect Emil B. Fetzer on the 1972 building, said plainly that the design team had no such symbolism in mind and that the effort went into the building's function rather than a scriptural reading of its exterior. Church News published a correction to this effect in 2024, citing historian Richard Cowan. The claim has the same texture as other retroactive prophecy-and-architecture readings: specific, satisfying, and absent from any account by the people who actually designed the building.
*Correct version:* The gold spire and white base were a functional design choice by Church architect Emil B. Fetzer. Any pillar-of-fire or pillar-of-cloud reading came from members after the fact, not from the design team.

## Salt Lake Temple

**"The main east doors are sealed until the Second Coming."**
Folklore. Not a Church teaching. Repeated on the Utah Geological Survey's downtown walking-tour page, among many others. In June 2026 the east doors were removed, restored over the course of the renovation, and rehung — not the treatment given to permanently sealed doors.
*Correct version:* Patrons historically entered through the annex on the north side rather than the ceremonial east doors.

**"45 years, 8 months, 9 days — the longest construction of any temple."**
Unsourceable. The oddly specific figure appears without citation and contradicts the Church's own framing.
*Correct version:* 40 years from the February 14, 1853 groundbreaking to the April 6, 1893 dedication. If a longer span is wanted, count from the July 28, 1847 site designation and label it as such.

**"Brigham Young had the pillars built hollow by revelation, foreseeing elevators."**
Urban myth. Elevator technology long predated the temple — Versailles had one in 1743. The shafts were left hollow because the builders intended to install elevators.

**"31 dedication sessions over 18 days."**
Arithmetic error. April 6 to April 24 spans 19 days, and sessions did not run on all of them.
*Correct version:* 31 sessions held between April 6 and 24, 1893. Services ran twice daily April 6–18, then again April 23–24, with April 21–22 reserved for Sunday School children.

**"Granite."**
The exterior is quartz monzonite from Little Cottonwood Canyon — granite in appearance, geologically distinct. Nearly every popular source says granite. Use the correct term; it is a small precision the audience notices.

**"A waterwheel in City Creek powered the Tabernacle organ."**
Legend, no documentation. The organ was hand-pumped until electrification. Adjacent to the temple story and sometimes told alongside it.

**Tunnel mythology.**
Tunnels exist and are used, but claims about a vast secret network reaching landmarks across downtown are unverified.

## St. George Utah Temple

**"The exterior stone was quarried from Little Cottonwood Canyon."**
Cross-contamination, not folklore. Little Cottonwood Canyon, near Salt Lake City, is where the Salt Lake Temple's quartz monzonite came from, roughly 300 miles north of St. George. Wikipedia's infobox for the St. George Utah Temple carries this claim, and it has every mark of a copy-paste error inherited from the Salt Lake Temple article rather than an independently sourced fact: hauling stone that distance by wagon in the 1870s would have been absurd, and the Church's own history site says plainly the stone came from north of St. George itself.
*Correct version:* The exterior is native red sandstone, quarried locally north of the city.

## General patterns

**Superlatives that have expired.** "Largest," "tallest," "only," "first in the world" are true as of a date. The temple count grows continuously and buildings are surpassed. Verify against a current Tier 1 or Tier 2 source and, where the claim is time-bound, write it that way: "largest by floor area" is safer than "largest temple."

**"Every temple has an angel Moroni."** Not true, and increasingly less true — a number of temples have never had one and some have had statues removed during renovation. Never assert a Moroni statue without confirming it for that specific building.

**"All Moroni statues face east."** Most do; not all. Confirm per building.

**Prophecy-fulfillment claims.** A building feature described as fulfilling a specific prophecy or revelation requires a primary source for the prophecy itself, not just a later writer's interpretation. These claims are also interpretive rather than factual, which puts them outside this brand's voice regardless of sourcing.

**Miraculous construction stories.** Divine intervention accounts — light phenomena, impossible timelines, materials appearing when needed — are frequently documented in journals as sincere accounts of subjective experience. That makes them real *as accounts*, not as verified events. Publish only if attributed as an account, with the source named, and only when the story is genuinely central. Usually better to omit.

**Round-number labor and cost figures.** "Took 10,000 men," "cost $4 million." Frequently invented or inflated in retelling. Require a Tier 1 or Tier 2 source.

**Freemasonry claims.** Assertions about Masonic derivation of temple symbols are contested and interpretive. Describe what the carvings *are* — clasped hands, all-seeing eye, sun and moon and star stones — without asserting their derivation.

**Ordinance-room detail.** Anything describing what happens inside beyond publicly documented room types and counts. Out of bounds for the brand irrespective of sourcing.

## Attribution traps

**Unsourced Brigham Young and Joseph Smith quotations.** A large volume of paraphrase and outright invention circulates under both names. Require a citation to a journal, letterbook, discourse, or Church publication. The genuine ones are usually better anyway — Young's "I want to see the Temple built in a manner that it will endure through the Millennium" is documented and does more work than any invented line.

**Quotations sourced only to a plaque, a tour guide, or a visitors' center display.** Not sufficient. Find the underlying document.

**Statistics with no attached source.** Attendance figures, visitor counts, and construction volumes propagate freely. Church Newsroom publishes open-house attendance for recent temples; use that.

## Adding to this file

When research turns up a claim that is widely repeated but cannot be sourced, add it here with the correction. The file is most valuable as it accumulates — the same handful of myths recur across many temples, and catching them on the second temple is cheaper than catching them after the fifth product page ships.

Format: the claim in bold, why it fails, then the corrected version if one exists.
