Washington D.C. Temple

Location
Kensington, Maryland. The temple carries the name of the capital it serves, but the building itself stands in suburban Kensington, Maryland, on a 52-acre site about 10 miles north of the U.S. Capitol. It rises in full view of the Capital Beltway (I-495), where millions of drivers have seen it since 1974.

Temple Number
The Church's 16th operating temple at its dedication, the first built in the Eastern United States and the Church's first east of the Mississippi River since the 1840s.

Announced
November 15, 1968. Ground was broken on the Kensington site just over three weeks later.

Groundbreaking
December 7, 1968. President Hugh B. Brown of the First Presidency presided. Full construction began May 28, 1971.

Dedicated
November 19, 1974, by President Spencer W. Kimball, in ten sessions held over four days through November 22, attended by some 40,000 members from across the eastern United States and eastern Canada.

Rededicated
August 14, 2022, by President Russell M. Nelson, in three sessions, following a renovation that had closed the temple since March 2018.

Architecture
Fred L. Markham, Henry P. Fetzer, Harold K. Beecher, and Keith W. Wilcox, architects, working under Church architect Emil B. Fetzer. Six-spire reinforced concrete structure sheathed in 173,000 square feet of Alabama white marble, 248 feet long by 136 feet wide, rising 288 feet to the top of the angel Moroni statue, with 156,558 square feet of floor area. It has been the tallest temple in the Church since its dedication.

Ordinance Rooms
Six instruction rooms, ten sealing rooms, and a baptistry with the font resting on twelve oxen representing the tribes of Israel.

First Temple President
Edward E. Drury Jr., a former Denver stake president who had just been released as president of the Delaware-Maryland Mission, called in 1973 ahead of the temple's 1974 opening.

Construction Story

  • The temple was announced on November 15, 1968 and the site broken three weeks later, but full construction waited until May 28, 1971. The finished building cost approximately $15 million, furnishings included.
  • The seven-week public open house in the fall of 1974 drew 758,328 visitors, breaking the record of 662,401 set by the Los Angeles California Temple. Demand for tickets forced organizers to expand from two phone lines to seventeen.
  • First Lady Betty Ford toured on September 11, 1974 and called the visit "a really, truly great experience." Vice President Nelson Rockefeller and Chief Justice Warren Burger also walked through before the dedication.
  • The Church held its largest news conference to date on the temple's 878-foot enclosed walkway bridge, with more than 100 reporters attending; coverage ultimately ran to 1,374 articles in 931 newspapers across all 50 states.

Changes Over Time

  • The temple closed March 5, 2018 for a renovation lasting more than four years: energy-efficient mechanical, electrical, and lighting systems, new plumbing, refreshed finishes and furnishings, and improved grounds.
  • Pandemic delays pushed the reopening celebration to 2022. The public open house ran April 28 to June 11, 2022, was extended to meet demand, and drew almost 350,000 guests, including U.S. senators, Supreme Court justices, and foreign ambassadors.
  • President Nelson, who rededicated the temple in 2022, had himself attended the original dedication in 1974.

Symbolism & Design

  • The six spires deliberately echo the Salt Lake Temple. Per the design intent the Church has published, the Salt Lake Temple represented the foundation of the Church and the Washington D.C. Temple its international future.
  • The spires are finished in 24-karat gold, and the 18-foot gold-leafed bronze angel Moroni by sculptor Avard T. Fairbanks makes this one of five temples whose Moroni statue holds the gold plates.
  • The exterior marble came from the quarry at Sylacauga, Alabama, and the windows combine stained glass with panels of translucent marble.

Trivia

  • Washington radio personalities came to use the temple as a reference point when announcing Beltway traffic, a sign it had taken its place among the monuments of the capital.
  • Since the 1970s the phrase "SURRENDER DOROTHY" has appeared again and again on the CSX railroad bridge that crosses the Beltway near the temple, a Wizard of Oz joke likening the temple's spires to the Emerald City. Highway crews repeatedly removed it, and in 2018 the bridge briefly read "SURRENDER DONALD" instead.
  • During the 1974 open house, President Spencer W. Kimball offered the invocation in the U.S. Senate and Elder Gordon B. Hinckley did the same in the House of Representatives on back-to-back days.
